# Narration Scripts: Segment 3

What a small clean sample of cited cells shows, and what a sample large enough to sign off on costs in time.

Voice notes for TTS:
- Conversational pace, a host thinking aloud with a colleague. Plain sentences, no rush.
- "Gemini" is read "JEM-in-eye". "Google Sheets" as two plain words.
- Percentages read in full, "forty-one percent", "nine and a half percent". "One in ten", "one in four" and "one time in twenty" are read as words.

## Segment 3: Five Clean Cells
*Source: `Segment3_Slides.pptx`*

### Slide 1

With thousands of values in a sheet, most teams settle the question the same way, and it feels responsible while we do it. Someone opens the first five companies on the list, clicks through to each cited page, and finds the value sitting there every time. Five out of five. The sheet goes into the board pack with a note that it has been checked, and everyone moves on to the next deadline. You've probably signed off on a sheet like this yourself, and the habit has a lot going for it. It's quick, it uses the citations the way they were meant to be used, and every one of those five values really was right. So the question is what those five clean cells tell us about the hundreds of cells nobody opened. Two things decide the answer. One is which five we chose, because a convenient five and a fair five can give very different answers. The other is how many we checked, because five is a small number, and small numbers can look like proof when they are mostly luck. Let's take the choice first, since it costs almost nothing to get right.

### Slide 2

The convenient five are usually the first five, and the start of a list is rarely a fair picture of what comes after it. A tutorial writer who teaches spreadsheet analysis calls checking only the first five or ten companies a classic mistake, and the reason is how lists get built. Whoever curates a supplier or prospect list puts the names they know first, and the names they know are the larger, better-documented companies. Those companies have current websites, recent filings and plenty of press coverage, so Gemini finds the right value for them more often. The small, privately held firms further down the list are where a stale directory entry or the wrong subsidiary's city slips in. So a check of the first five companies tests the easiest values we have and reports the result as if it covered the hardest. The remedy is a decision to choose at random, and it costs a few clicks. He gives every company on the list a random number, freezes those numbers so they stop changing, sorts by them, and checks whatever lands first. Now every value has the same chance of being opened, and five clean cells say something about the whole list instead of its easiest stretch. The next question is how much they say.

### Slide 3

Suppose one cell in ten across the whole list is wrong, and we open five at random. Each cell we open has a nine in ten chance of being clean, so five clean draws in a row happen about six times in ten. That means the check finds at least one error only about forty-one percent of the time. Most of the time, a list with a real problem passes a five-cell check without a mark against it. And that pass looks exactly like the pass we would get from a list with no errors at all, because the wrong values carry the same confident text and the same tidy citation as the right ones. On a short list the arithmetic shifts a little. With twenty companies and two wrong values, every clean cell we draw leaves fewer clean cells behind, so the chance of catching an error rises to about forty-five percent. That is still worse than a coin toss. The sentence worth keeping is that a five-cell check misses a one-in-ten problem more often than it catches it. So a clean result from five cells is weak evidence, and the useful next step is to ask exactly how weak.

### Slide 4

Let's turn the question around, because the one a manager asks after a clean result is how bad the list could still be. Five cells came back clean. What is the highest error rate that would still hand us five clean cells at least one time in twenty? The answer is forty-five percent. A list where almost half the values are wrong will pass a five-cell check about once in every twenty tries, so a clean five cannot rule it out. The five clean cells we signed off on are consistent with almost half the list being wrong. Adding cells pulls that ceiling down, slowly. At ten clean cells it sits near twenty-six percent. The tutorial writer we met a moment ago puts it plainly: ten clean checks still leave room for up to about one wrong cell in three. His rule of thumb runs a little high, and the careful figure is closer to one in four, though the point holds either way. At twenty clean cells the ceiling is about fourteen percent. Only at thirty clean cells does it fall under ten percent, to about nine and a half. Thirty is where a clean result starts to mean what we hoped five meant.

### Slide 5

Every cited cell we open takes under a minute to read against its page, so a bigger sample costs us minutes and buys a better chance of catching a problem. Five random cells take about five minutes and catch a one-in-ten error rate forty-one percent of the time. That makes five a smoke test, good for spotting a broken prompt before we spend a day on it, and poor grounds for a sign-off. Ten cells take about ten minutes and lift the catch rate to sixty-five percent. Twenty cells, about twenty minutes, reach eighty-eight percent. Twenty-nine cells is where the chance of catching a one-in-ten problem first passes ninety-five percent, and thirty cells, about half an hour, give ninety-six percent along with the ceiling under ten percent we just worked out. Thirty random cells is about half an hour, and that half hour is what "under ten percent" costs. Which columns deserve that half hour, and which deserve more, is the next thing to settle.
